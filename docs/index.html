01 · The problem

Score every customer call for satisfaction and sentiment, and know how far to trust each score.

For contact-centre operations and quality leads in lending and loan servicing. Today they hand-review a sliver of calls and hear back from the few customers who answer a survey, who skew unhappy. They need a score on every call, the transcript lines behind it, and an honest measure of how much it can be trusted.

Synthetic data 400 generated calls, two simulated annotators and simulated surveys. Every number on this page measures the system on that data, not on real callers.

02 · The approach

A model extracts facts. Code turns them into scores.

What it does

Each call gets a satisfaction score from 1 to 5 and a sentiment of happy, mild or not happy, each with a one-line explanation and the transcript lines that produced it. Calls too broken to score go to a human review queue instead. Scores roll up by agent, intent and day.

Why it is built this way

A model asked for “satisfaction, 1 to 5” gives an opinion nobody can audit or retune. Here the model only answers narrow, typed questions (was the request resolved, how many times did the caller repeat themselves, which turn shows it). Deterministic code does the scoring, so every score is reproducible, explainable and testable.

How it works

  1. 1Quality gateSpeaker labels, ASR confidence, length, dropped calls. Failures go to review, never scored.
  2. 2Split by requestOne call often carries several requests; each is judged on its own turns.
  3. 3ExtractTyped fields with evidence spans, from Jev or a rule-based backend.
  4. 4Score in codeSatisfaction from resolution and effort. Sentiment from what the caller said.
  5. 5CalibrateMap the score onto the survey scale, corrected for who answers surveys.

03 · Results

At the human ceiling on satisfaction. Weak on finding the quiet churn risk.

Measured on the sealed holdout of 100 calls, evaluated once per system, unless marked otherwise. Intervals are 95% bootstrap over calls.

Agreement with humans, against the human ceiling

Two annotators labelled 300 calls. How often they agree with each other is the ceiling: a system that agrees with a human as well as humans agree with each other has nothing left to gain from more prompt tuning. 200 calls were used to iterate; 100 were sealed and evaluated once per system (the ledger refuses a second look).

Confusion matrices and the noise floor

Satisfied is not the same as polite

Satisfaction comes from resolution and effort; sentiment comes from what the caller expressed. Scoring them from separate evidence exposes the cell a single blended score hides: callers whose problem was not solved but who stayed polite. They do not complain on the call, and they are the churn risk.

Churn-risk calls

Satisfaction 1–2 with sentiment happy or mild. Select one to open it in the explorer.

    Calibration: a score is a ranking until it is anchored

    The raw score is fit to post-call survey answers with a monotone (isotonic) map. Unhappy callers answer surveys more often, so each answer is weighted by the inverse response rate of its score band. Error is measured out of fold, never on the calls used to fit.

      05 · Limits and next steps

      What this does not show yet

      • Synthetic data. Calls come from a closed set of templates with ASR noise on top, so agreement numbers are optimistic for any extractor. On real calls the human ceiling will be lower than 0.85 and must be measured.
      • The rules are not a result. The rule-based extractor matches Jev here because its lexicons were written against the same templates. The paired dev difference is within noise; on real paraphrase expect the rules to fall away.
      • The quiet churn risk is under-found. Polite, unresolved callers are flagged with no false alarms on the holdout, but most are missed.
      • Calibration rests on 83 surveys. It helps the Jev scores and slightly hurts the rule scores, whose raw scale was already close. Until calibrated error is clearly below raw, publish buckets, not numbers.
      • Adjudication is simulated as recovering the true label. The like-for-like comparison (system vs one annotator) does not depend on it.
      • One disclosed leak. An eight-call smoke test of the first question version included two holdout calls; the changes that followed were generic. Details in METHODOLOGY.md.

      Now

      • Raise churn-risk recall: inspect missed dev calls, change one thing, keep it only if a paired comparison clears both noise floors.
      • Tighten Jev intent precision: require a caller turn the model cites before a model-only request is scored.

      Next

      • Score-distribution drift alert against a reference window.
      • A full night of about 20,000 calls in shadow, published as buckets until the reliability diagram sits near the diagonal.

      Later

      • Replace synthetic data with a frozen sample of real calls and real annotators; measure the ceiling before any extraction work.
      • Fit calibration on real surveys and recalibrate on a schedule.
